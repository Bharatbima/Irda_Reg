# Affinity Business Under the New Regime — Unbundling Thesis

**Core thesis:** Affinity/credit-linked insurance distribution doesn't disappear under the reform — it restructures. NBFCs/banks currently running this in-house at ~42% effective payout (Part 1, paras 43-45) cannot survive on the new capped economics (Box 4A/4B Section E: ~2-5% on loan-linked covers) because their fixed cost base (branch RMs, incentive structures) was sized for the old margin. They will exit or shrink this function. The underlying customer need and NBFC commercial interest do not go away — only who can profitably serve it changes.

**The margin-recovery mechanism (Section 10):**
- Loan-linked minimum cover (compulsory-adjacent, e.g. property/life tied directly to the loan) is capped low under Section E — Property multi-year 5%, Life pure-term multi-year 2.5%/1% renewal.
- Section 10's "acceptable package" safe list requires: an informed with/without-insurance comparison shown to the customer, no lender lock-in, premium paid separately from the loan amount. This structurally forces a second, separately consented transaction for any additional/voluntary cover.
- That voluntary individual layer reverts to full standalone individual commission (Box 4B): Individual Pure Term single-premium 7.5% (IDE), etc.
- Net effect: a compliant "5% (capped loan-linked) + 7.5% (voluntary individual top-up)" blended structure of roughly 12.5% — versus the old opaque ~42% bundled NBFC take, but a real, survivable number versus a bare 2% floor.

**Two distinct layers:**

- **Layer 2 — the para-131 master/group policy:** the lending institution insures its own loan book, paying premium as its own institutional expense. Here the NBFC is the customer, not a distributor — no retail sale occurs, so none of Sections 9/11's distributor obligations (suitability documentation, PIR seller-tagging, verified-connect) are triggered by this transaction, whether the NBFC goes straight to an insurer's commercial desk or via a broker. If via broker: the broker carries broker-side obligations (Section 8 disclosure/certification) as its own cost of business. There is no compliance-avoidance value-add in servicing Layer 2 on the NBFC's behalf — the value has to come from somewhere else (see Opportunity 1).

- **Layer 1 — the borrower-facing unbundled cover:** this is a genuine distribution event to a retail customer, and the NBFC is already today's distributor here — Part 1 confirms NBFCs currently earn their ~42% effective payout acting as corporate agents. Under the new regime, if the NBFC continues doing this itself, it inherits Section 9's verified-connect build, Section 11's suitability/PIR-tagging/claw-back regime, and Section 8's disclosure/cost-audit thresholds if crossed. This compliance-outsourcing case is strongest for NBFCs without their own retail infrastructure already in place (see Opportunity 2).

## OPPORTUNITY 1 — Master Policy Handling (MPH) service

**What it is:** service the Layer 2 master/group policy for CA-licensed NBFCs — placement, renewal negotiation, and critically, claims administration — for institutions where running it in-house isn't worth the operational bandwidth at a ~2% margin.

**Why it's real:** at 2%, this isn't worth an NBFC's hassle to manage itself, but the moment of truth is claims — when an adverse event triggers a payout on the portfolio cover, whoever handles that smoothly and gets it paid is who the NBFC keeps. This is:
- Manual, not automatable. Claims on a commercial portfolio policy are judgment-heavy, relationship-driven, bespoke — no API replaces this.
- A service-quality moat, not a price or tech moat. The USP is reliable, fast claims resolution — harder for a competitor to replicate than a placement rate, and a small, high-touch, motivated team can outperform a slow insurer claims desk on exactly this.
- Thin percentage (Box 4B: Group Pure Term 1.5-5%, Group Fund Based 0.5%) but on a large premium base (a lender's whole loan book) — viable on absolute revenue if claims service is genuinely good enough to retain the mandate.

**Capability requirement:** this opportunity depends on Bharat Bima having (or building) real claims-handling capability and insurer relationships strong enough to make the service credible.

## OPPORTUNITY 2 — Cross-sell process management for smaller societies/NBFCs

**What it is:** manage the retail cross-sell process (Layer 1) specifically for a smaller set of cooperative societies and NBFCs, not a broad takeover of all Layer 1 distribution. NBFCs with genuine existing retail lending infrastructure (branches, DSAs, staff already at the point of loan disbursal) will keep doing insurance cross-sell themselves even at reduced margin, because the marginal cost of cross-selling on already-sunk retail infrastructure is near zero. The addressable segment is smaller institutions — particularly cooperative societies — that don't have that kind of infrastructure sunk already, or where scale doesn't justify running full IDE compliance in-house.

**The institution-payment structuring problem:**

Section 6 limits the entire distribution universe to three registered types — IDE, IDP, MII. Nobody outside that structure is legally entitled to be paid for facilitating insurance distribution. This creates a real problem for this opportunity:

1. **Section 8, Principle 12 (all-inclusive commission definition):** any payment "by any name whatsoever" connected to the distribution — referral fee, access fee, marketing fee — counts as commission and must fit inside the capped ceiling, payable only to a licensed party.
2. **Section 8, paras 113-115 (related-party safeguards):** payment to an entity connected to the business placed requires Board approval on both sides, a "no linkage whatsoever" confirmation, and advance regulator disclosure. A plain "we pay you X% for member access" arrangement looks exactly like the disguised commission-sharing this clause targets.
3. **The one clean legal path:** cooperative societies are explicitly named as an eligible IDE legal structure (Section 6, para 92.1.A — listed alongside companies, LLPs, registered partnerships, trusts). An NBFC is already IDE-eligible as a registered company. The institution can become its own licensed IDE and earn commission directly, or its individual staff can register as IDPs (Insurance Associates explicitly covers SHGs/proprietorships/unregistered partnerships, which may fit smaller society structures) — but there is no clean middle path where Bharat Bima simply pays the institution for access without one of those two registrations happening.

**Practical implication:** this opportunity requires Bharat Bima to either (a) help the society/NBFC itself register and operate as a compliant IDE, with Bharat Bima providing back-office/placement/compliance support to that IDE as a service-fee relationship rather than a distribution-commission-sharing one, or (b) onboard the institution's own staff as registered IDPs under Bharat Bima's IDE umbrella, with commission flowing to them individually per the standard IDP rules, not to the institution as an entity. This requires real legal/compliance structuring work, not something resolved by document-reading alone.

---

**Additional structural argument:** broker fiduciary duty vs. corporate-agent/direct duty-to-insurer (para 86c) remains a real, independent reason to route business through a broker in both Layer 1 and Layer 2 contexts.

**Two compliance design constraints to build in from day one (mainly Layer 1 / Opportunity 2):**
1. **Related-party risk (Section 8, paras 113-115)** — central to Opportunity 2's payment problem, above.
2. **PoSP cost-stacking (Section 8, Principle 13):** commissions to PoSPs engaged by an IDE come out of the IDE's own capped commission, not additive. On thin margins this could eliminate the model.

**Open questions:**
- Does Bharat Bima have, or need to build, claims-handling capability and insurer relationships strong enough to make MPH's claims-service value proposition credible?
- Which specific structuring route (society/NBFC as its own IDE vs. staff as IDPs under Bharat Bima) is realistic given the small institutions being targeted, and whether they have the appetite/capacity for either.
- Whether the "5%+7.5% unbundled" blended economics (the original Layer 1 thesis) remains the right frame, or whether Opportunity 2 has superseded it for the smaller-institution segment.
- Concentration risk: leaning into MPH and small-institution cross-sell together risks overweighting Bharat Bima toward the lending-institution channel — worth treating as one vertical among several rather than the sole bet.
- Whether Bharat Bima's current track record supports winning MPH or IDE-support mandates today, or whether this is a credibility-building-phase opportunity.

**Status:** two concrete, reasonably well-scoped opportunities (MPH service; small-society/NBFC cross-sell management). Both need a capability check against what Bharat Bima can deliver today, and real legal structuring work on the institution-payment problem, before either moves from thesis to plan.
