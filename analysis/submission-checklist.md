**Q9.** The proposed structure allows insurance distributors and persons to supply non-insurance products and non-financial services also. Is there a need for safeguards against poor market conduct?
**Tag: ANSWER (draft direction).** Non-insurance cross-sell is the one surviving Section 6 opportunity, with the caveat of "how much it pulls from core broking." **Q10.** The proposed structure provides an opening for innovative distribution business models to come in without the need to develop special categories through regulatory revisions. However, does the proposed structure adequately accommodate all existing and emerging distribution models, or are there categories of distributors whose business models may fall outside the framework?
**Tag: ANSWER — strongest candidate in this section.** This is where the embedded/API-at-transaction-point pattern (NBFC Layer 1, tractor/OEM) has real standing to raise: the IDE/IDP/MII trichotomy and the "acceptable package" safe list (Section 10) are built around traditional human-intermediary and institutional-bundling scenarios. A zero-touch, API-embedded insurance offer made at the moment of an already-happening transaction (loan disbursal, vehicle delivery) doesn't map cleanly onto either "distributor pushing a product" or "MII pull-based marketplace" — it's a third pattern the paper doesn't name. Worth flagging as a genuine structural gap/ambiguity, not necessarily asking for a new category, but seeking clarity that such models are accommodated under existing IDE/IDP registration without additional friction.

**Q15.** Does the sector need a longer timeframe to achieve the proposed norms?
**Tag: ANSWER.** Direct evidence this matters unevenly: the glide path (General 25%→20%, Life 15%→12.5% over 5 years) hits insurers very differently depending on starting point — a 39%-CoDB insurer facing the same 5-year runway to 20% as a 25%-CoDB insurer is a much steeper compression. From Bharat Bima's seat, faster compression at high-EoM insurers is actually favorable (accelerates the shift toward disciplined insurers preferred to place business with), so our interest may run *against* a longer timeframe, or neutral. This is an insurer-facing question, not directly ours to lobby on — but the "same runway, different starting points" issue is worth noting as a fairness/practicality observation without taking a side on timeframe length.

## Distribution Commission Framework (Section 8)
**Q16.** Has open architecture served its purpose or is it simply increasing costs without adding any significant value for the end customer? Is there more that can be done in this area than proposed in the consultation paper.
**Tag: ANSWER.** Direct practitioner ground-truth here (Section 6 corrected log): open architecture is already the de facto default for brokers, and PoSP exclusivity is trivially circumvented via re-registration under another name. Real-world evidence: Part 2 data shows PoSP-led brokers' commissions climbing steeply (FY23→FY25, e.g. one broker 6%→22%→24%) alongside premium growth of only 1.37x vs commission growth of 2.73x — i.e., "open architecture" in practice has correlated with rising cost, not rising customer value. Credible, evidence-backed answer: open architecture in its current unenforced form has added cost without proportionate value; real value only shows up when architecture is paired with actual transparency/accountability (Section 9), not architecture alone.

**Q17.** Are the principles proposed for the distribution commission reforms appropriate?
**Tag: PASS** (default) — plumbing-level question about the twelve principles in Section 8; no strong differentiated view beyond what's already used to build the model theses.

**Q18.** Is the sector ready to move from the current arrangement of opaque commissions paid by insurers and unknown to policyholders to a new arrangement of distribution based on fee paid by policyholders directly?
**Tag: ANSWER — strong candidate.** Squarely the commercial-lines thesis territory. Part 2 evidence already shows non-PoSP, non-commission-chasing brokers running 30-60% PAT margins on just 6-11% commission serving corporates — i.e., a lower-commission, higher-transparency, advisory-fee-adjacent model already works in the market today, ahead of any regulatory mandate. Can answer in the affirmative with evidence: yes, at least for complex/commercial lines, the market has already proven readiness; retail/mass-market readiness is a separate, harder question not positioned to comment on.

**Q19.** Do the proposed commission limits arrive at a fair balance between policyholders' expectations of affordability and distributors' expectations of adequate remuneration?
**Tag: ANSWER.** Hard current-state data to ground this: Table 5 shows Corporate Agent-NBFC commissions currently ranging 16-88% (avg) with 88% max — the proposed caps represent a severe compression from this baseline. Grounded point (from the affinity-model thesis): "no business can work at the compressed rate" is a real economic constraint at the very low end — worth flagging that a viable floor matters as much as an affordable ceiling, without arguing against the caps' direction.

**Q20.** Within the proposed commission limits, which are the possible measures to incentivize good market conduct? Vice-versa, what measures can be built in to disincentivize bad market conduct?
**Tag: ANSWER.** Direct tie to the Section 15 opportunity: proactive, voluntary disclosure of commission structure and customer-facing performance (ahead of any mandate) is itself a positive-conduct signal IRDAI could recognize/reward — e.g., via preferred treatment in EoM flexibility or public recognition mechanisms. Doubles as a chance to put Bharat Bima's own planned practice on record in the submission itself.

**Q21.** Compliance to commission structure has to be at the insurer and distributor level. Proper and timely reconciliation of commissions is necessary so that the "commission paid and commission received" are identical amounts. How can various regulated entities be made more responsible and compliant?
**Tag: PASS.** Operational/insurer-side plumbing question, no differentiated broker-side view.

## Safeguards against Compulsory Bundling (Section 10)
**Q24.** Are the illustrations of acceptable packaging of insurance with credit or loan appropriate?
**Tag: ANSWER — direct hit.** This is the exact mechanism the affinity-model-unbundling-thesis.md is built on. Grounded, specific view: the safe-list illustrations (complimentary group cover, credit-card-bundled cover, conditional interest-rate discount with disclosure) are workable in principle, but the practical difficulty is in Layer 1 (borrower-facing individual cover) economics once margins compress to the capped range — the "acceptable package" framework is clear on what's allowed, less clear on whether the economics of running it *compliantly* (via broker or NBFC-as-CA) remain viable at the new caps. Worth citing the 5%+7.5% unbundling logic as evidence of the real-world adaptation this forces.
