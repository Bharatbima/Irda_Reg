## Section 2 — Introduction (paras 23–33)

- **Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha Act, 2025 (SBSR Act)** — anchors four objectives: (1) accelerate sector growth, (2) better policyholder protection, (3) improve ease of doing business, (4) transparency in regulation-making and oversight.
- **Current Status:** industry growth tracks GDP rather than outpacing it; large unmet protection needs.
  - Life: top 2 insurers = 65% market share; bottom 15 = 5%.
  - General: top 10 = 68%; remaining 24 = 32%.
  - Policy count has been broadly stagnant (life); general insurance grew but not enough.
- **Cost of Doing Business (CoDB)** (= total expenses / total premium or GDPI):
  - Life (ex-top-2): 22% (range 18–39%). Top-2: 10–12%.
  - General (ex-largest): 29% (range 20–48%). Largest (13% share): 18%.
- **RBI Financial Stability Report (Dec 2025)** flagged: high expense structure persisting; premium growth driven by high-cost distribution not efficiency/digital; frontloaded acquisition costs in life; high acquisition costs + claims inflation in general causing underwriting losses; constrained coverage expansion. Recommends moving from "high-cost, low-inclusion" to "affordable-cost, broad inclusion, high quality."
- **Economic Survey 2025-26**: flags escalating acquisition cost, high operating costs, dependence on expensive intermediary networks vs digital. Notes divergence between coverage "depth" (revenue from existing customers) vs "breadth" (new risk pool) — sector is deepening, not widening.

## Section 3 — Challenges of the Insurance Sector (paras 34–76)

Ten numbered structural challenges — this is the diagnostic spine of the whole paper.

**1) Growth hasn't kept pace with need.** Coverage/penetration stagnant despite economic growth. 'Missing middle', informal workers, MSMEs remain under-protected. Health: top 6 insurers = 65-68% market; 10 states = 80-82% of retail health; Maharashtra alone = 20%. Life: 66-70% of retail new business in 10 states. Property: often purchased only when lender-mandated, lapses once loan requirement ends.

**2) Interests of insurers, distributors, public are misaligned.** Remuneration rewards premium procurement over suitability/persistency. Corporate agency channel (life): NB premium +28% (FY23–25) vs distributor remuneration **+125%**. Remuneration now ~27% of first-year premium, with rewards/incentives adding **30-60% over base commission**. ULIP payouts 5-40%; other savings products 29-60% (up to 65%+). NBFC corporate agency channel nearly tripled since FY23 (~42% avg payout; 93% is single-premium Group Credit Life at loan origination).

**3) Incentives reward growth, not value.** 61st-month persistency only **48%** overall vs **71%** for online channel. General insurance grievances: 78,347 (FY23) → 1,37,361 (FY25); 69% relate to claims.

**4) Sales are commission-led, not price/quality-led.** General insurance broker channel: premium +37% (FY23–25) vs commission **+173%**; avg broker commission 8.5%→17%. Motor commissions ~9%→25%; retail health ~10%→30%. Motor Third Party (regulated/mandatory) commission 4.3%→22%. Some long-term fire/health (de-tariffed) commissions near 100% (paid upfront on multi-year premium — EoM/capital strain). Life FYP commissions: highs near 80%, average up to 50%.

**5) High cost + uncertain service quality weakening trust.** FY26 Bima Bharosa: 63% of disposed complaints settled in favour of policyholder; Ombudsman level 75% in favour; consumer forums/courts 54% in favour.

**6) Large/organised distributors' leverage constrains insurer options.** Banks: ~₹68,000 cr sampled corporate agency premium; multi-tie-up banks paid more than single-tie-up. Health: some corporate/group arrangements reach **93%** payout. Motor: ~49% sourced via brokers; OEM/dealer channels demand up to **40%** commission on new vehicles.

**7) Regulatory arbitrage — non-level playing field.** Different entry/capital/compliance requirements for entities doing substantially similar functions.

**8) Lack of transparency on performance** of products/insurers/distributors limits informed purchase. General insurance channel mix: brokers 33% + individual agents 30% of premium; direct online only ~2%; web aggregators negligible — despite offering best comparison scope.

**9) Customer power absent in an imperfect market.** Information asymmetry: pricing, claims track record, and returns on savings products not comparable across insurers.

**10) 2023 EoM reforms haven't delivered.** Costs back to pre-2016 highs (see Box 1, Section 7). Commission costs risen sharply in general insurance, especially intermediated business; life saw sharp NBFC-registration increase + commission rise across channels. Three design flaws identified: (a) EoM computed on total GWP including reinsurance/govt business creates headroom for high intermediated payouts within overall limit; (b) Board-approved commission policies don't clearly justify remuneration differences; (c) health standalone insurers channelled underwriting margins into commissions/opex rather than lower premiums.

## Section 4 — Distribution Reforms: A Compelling Priority (paras 77–83)

- The 10 challenges are "manifestations of a common underlying problem": architecture rewards premium collection over value/expansion/efficiency.
- Frames distribution reform as an **economic priority, not merely a regulatory exercise** — "second generation reforms" focused on distribution efficiency, market conduct, customer outcomes — not just commission/intermediary tweaks.
- Digital platforms + interoperable public infrastructure + data-driven underwriting cited as the enabling opportunity, contingent on regulatory norms evolving alongside.

## Section 5 — Strategic Framework for Reforms (para 84)

The **10 objectives** anchoring everything from Section 6 onward:

1. **Eliminate regulatory arbitrage** — same structure → same entry/capital/scope/obligations/regulation. Also: money should move where it belongs (customer → insurer directly per Sec 64VB); direct verified connect between customer and insurer before policy issuance.
2. **Remove restrictions on non-insurance business** for intermediaries — one-stop-shop reduces push to over-sell insurance for income.
3. **Ease entry and capital requirements** — distributors don't carry underwriting risk, so solvency-margin-style capital isn't warranted; capital needed mainly for going-concern operations.
4. **Reduce total expenses with a glide path.**
5. **Cap commissions/rewards** on an effort-and-complexity framework, aligned to removing arbitrage — explicit statement that EoM flexibility "has not succeeded" so **hard caps, strict implementation, sharp oversight** is "now the way forward."
6. **Set up Market Infrastructure Institutions (MIIs)** for a level playing field.
7. **Create incentives for good market conduct.**
8. **Build in disincentives for bad conduct** — penal action, not just compliance minimum.
9. **Ensure transparency by market design**, not as an option or through enforcement.
10. **Mitigate structural/operational friction to customer power** — frictionless journeys, comparison, switching, grievance redressal.

## Section 6 — Simplification of Architecture and Scope of Distribution (paras 85–92)

**Current fragmentation (para 86–90):**
- **8 entity types**: corporate agents, brokers, composite brokers, OEM brokers, IMFs, web aggregators, common service centres, MISP dealers.
- **9 individual-seller types**: agents, PoSPs, specified persons, designated persons, broker qualified person, rural authorized person, village level entrepreneurs, authorized verifier, and (per Annexure) associated categories.
- Key variances flagged: open vs closed architecture access; capital (brokers ₹75L–5cr; IMFs/web aggregators lower; corporate agents/MISPs — none); fiduciary duty differs (broker → customer; corporate agent → insurer, but policyholder unaware of either's pay); scope (brokers unrestricted; corporate agents capped at ₹5cr sum assured for commercial; IMFs product/geo restricted); non-insurance activity allowed for IMFs/banks but not brokers; training 15–75 hours with only 35% pass threshold.

**7 Simplification Principles (para 91):**
1. Same regulation for same legal structure + scope of business
2. Clear distinction: open architecture vs closed architecture
3. Expand non-competing business opportunities but reorient commission toward public benefit
4. Enhance knowledge/skill requirements; easier consistent training/testing
5. Reduce capital requirements for entry, linked to subsequent business volume
6. Set regulatory expectations cogently
7. Promote accountability/transparency by design

**Reforms (para 92), the actual proposal:**

1. **Three distributor types only:**
   - **IDE (Insurance Distribution Entity)** — companies/LLPs/cooperatives/registered partnerships/trusts. Absorbs corporate agents, brokers, composite brokers, OEM brokers, IMFs, web aggregators, CSC-SPV. Business correspondent companies also allowed to register as IDE.
   - **IDP (Insurance Distribution Person)** — individuals/employees/proprietorships/unregistered partnerships. Only **4 names going forward**: (i) Insurance Agents (incl. individual business correspondents), (ii) Insurance Associates (proprietorships, unregistered partnerships, SHGs, HUFs), (iii) Specified Persons (IDE employees), (iv) PoSPs (engaged by any IDE). Current PoSPs/MISPs with entity-type structure must register as IDE — they stop being PoSPs.
   - **MII (Market Infrastructure Institution)** — not-for-profit, insurer-promoted. Bima Sugam is the first example.

2. **Capital requirements (reduced, linked to volume):**
   - IDE: ₹10 lakh initial capital; **plus** 0.1% of prior year's commission/insurance income as a bank deposit (lien to IRDAI), min ₹10 lakh, max ₹10 crore, reset every 3 years, funded from capital/accruals; positive net worth every year.
   - MII: ₹25 crore capital.

3. **Registration simplified:** IDE/MII registration becomes **permanent** (subject to annual fee) instead of 3-year renewal cycle (already notified, effective FY27-28). No more approval requirement for "specified persons" of corporate agents — no registration/approval needed for IDPs by the Authority at all. Centralized sector-wide functional identity for IDPs to be set up on an MII with PIR backend support.

4. **Uniform fee structure:** Application-cum-registration fee ₹10,000; annual fee = higher of ₹10,000 or **0.04%** of prior-year commission/receipts.

5. **Scope of business uniform within type:** All IDEs get the *option* (not obligation) of open architecture — multi-insurer, unlimited products, unlimited risk cover — subject to having adequately trained IDPs.

6. **Sharper open/closed distinction:**
   - IDPs engaged by insurers → **closed architecture only**. Can represent one life, one general, one health insurer (and one of each other mono-line), but not two *competing* products (e.g., can't sell both a general insurer's health product and a health insurer's health product).
   - IDPs engaged by IDEs (Specified Persons, PoSPs) → can only sell that IDE's products. **PoSPs cannot work with multiple IDEs.**

7. **Non-competing business expansion:** All IDEs/IDPs may sell non-insurance financial products (subject to that sector regulator's approval) and non-financial products/services (subject to that sector's mandate if required).

8. **Qualification/training raised:**
   - New IDPs: minimum **Class XII** (those in profession <5 yrs without it, encouraged via NIOS correspondence).
   - Training: minimum **100 hours** self+assisted learning per segment (life/general), preferably online. Those with 5+ years' experience: shorter refresher + online test.
   - Life/General Insurance Councils mandated to build free, public-good model training content; IDEs may add product-specific modules.
   - Testing: secure online tests, available down to taluka/block level (CSCs can host).

9. **Regulatory Do's/Don'ts explicitly set out:**
   - **IDEs — Do's:** accountability for IDP actions; organise IDP training; onboard IDPs on industry-wide platform; obtain unique IDP functional IDs; assign one IDP per outlet; tag each policy with the selling IDP's ID; facilitate customer authentication; ensure adequate risk disclosure; provide full risk-parameter info to insurers for commercial insurance.
   - **IDEs — Don'ts:** no same-pricing-on-all-channels insistence; no handling customer money; no direct commission/fee collection from customers unless specified; no assigning one IDP to more than one outlet.
   - **Specified Persons/PoSPs — Do's:** online training/testing/certification; consent for verification of qualifications; provide Aadhaar+PAN at onboarding; disclose functional identity/contact to customers; suitability analysis before offering products; (PoSPs earning ₹20 lakh+/yr) standing consent for PIR to pull GST details from GSTN. **Don't:** handle customer money.
   - **Agents/Associates of Insurers — Do's:** same training/consent/ID/suitability requirements; **must offer the most suitable product, not the higher-commission one**; (₹20 lakh+/yr) GST consent to PIR. **Don't:** handle customer money.

10. **New distribution channels allowed:** Hospitals can register as IDEs to distribute health insurance (safeguards + lower commission limits). Non-dealer automobile garages can sell motor insurance as an insurer's associate. **TPAs and Surveyors explicitly barred** from selling insurance (claim-support-only entities).

11. **Insurers as distributors, with safeguards:** An insurer may distribute its *group* (non-competing) insurer's products through its own branch offices, via its own employees only (not via any IDE/IDP) — at lower commission.

## Section 7 — Expenses of Management (EoM) Framework (paras 93–97)

- Diagnosis: 2023 EoM framework was "conceptually sound" (shift from commission caps → entity-level expense discipline, Board accountability) but "has under-delivered" — expenses kept rising, more pronounced in private insurers.
- 2023 framework's extra carve-outs (tech spend, insurance awareness, rural/govt scheme expenses) blurred in practice with routine license/AMC spend — **proposed to fold back into the overall EoM ceiling.**

**Box 1 — Total Expense Ratio (historical arc):**
- Life (private): 21.3% (FY15) → low of 16.5% (FY21) → **20.2% (FY26)**, "nearly back to where the decade began."
- General (private): 30.3% (FY15) → ~25% (FY19) → **32.1% (FY26)**, "exceeding even its pre-2016 level."
- Explicitly attributed to progressive relaxation of expense controls culminating in the 2023 entity-level EoM framework: "The discipline achieved over a decade has been unwound in the space of a few years and it is policyholders who have funded the reversal."

**Reform measures (para 95):**
1. **EoM basis changes**: computed as Gross Expenses / **GDPI** (Gross Direct Premium Income), not GWP (Gross Written Premium) — eliminates inward reinsurance double-counting. Gross Commission (GC) continues to be included. **Life insurance: EoM = Total Expenses / Total Premium.**
2. Reinsurance cession payments (other than claims) received by a cedant from a reinsurer = **reinsurance commission**, cannot be netted against expenses/commissions for EoM purposes.
3. **Box 2 — EoM Limits & Glide Path (the headline numbers):**
   - **Life:** 15% of premium in 2 years, **12.5% in 5 years** (FY2027-28 = Year 1, annual reduction required). Those already below FY25 benchmark must reach **10% in 5 years** (stated as "the goal of the sector in the long-term").
   - **General:** 25% of premium in 2 years, **20% in 5 years** (FY2027-28 = Year 1). Those already below benchmark must reach 20% in 5 years too.
   - Note: paper explicitly says these EoM ratios were "of the same lower order 6 to 8 years back."
4. **Regulatory fee cut**: 0.05% → **0.04%** of premium, capped at ₹20 crore — reduces fee for all except 5 insurers with premium up to ₹37,500 crore.
5. Non-commission operating expense variance flagged: FY26 range 6.1–27.7% (life) and 6.8–28.8% of GDPI (general) — insurers must also control non-commission opex.
6. **PMFBY (crop insurance) carve-out**: only **1/3rd of Gross Premium** counts as GDPI for EoM computation — because acquisition cost isn't proportional to premium and states use cup-and-cap models capping insurer liability at 130% of premium (flagged as a growing systemic risk to state governments/farmers in extreme weather events).
7. **Mandatory cost audit**: all insurers regardless of size; **IDEs with insurance revenue >₹100 crore** also required.
8. **Disclosure mandate**: IDEs with insurance revenue >₹50 crore must publicly declare (on website) revenue, expenses, related-party payments, PAT, etc.
- Review point: EoM norms to be reviewed alongside product reforms likely in **FY28**.
- Non-compliance consequences (para 97): graduated — restrictions on new product launches → dividend distribution restrictions → in extreme cases, new-business restrictions on the offending channel.

## Section 8 — Distribution Commission Framework (paras 98–115)

- Frames the market as "imperfect" despite many players — market failures from lack of transparency in (1) product/pricing/claims performance, (2) risk disclosure, (3) distribution/operating costs. This paper addresses cause (3) only; other causes to be tackled in future sequential reforms.
- Explicitly: "the moot point is readiness... to disclose commission rates on policy documents or alternatively shifting to a system of **fee paid by customers instead of commission by insurers**." (Signals a future direction, not yet proposed as mandatory.)
- States 2023/2024 Board-approval-based framework has "often been a formality rather than being substantive" — justifying **hard caps**.

**Box 3 — Operating Expenses & Commissions (FY26 data cited):**
- Private life insurers: avg 9% commission on premium; but largest player (10% share) paid only 4%; second largest 11%; range 3–39%. Public sector life insurer (55% share, mostly agents): 5% avg. Top-4 insurers' total expense+commission range 11–21%; rest ~25%.
- Private general insurers: avg >20% commission; range 15–31% (higher in specific lines). Public sector general insurers: 11% avg.

**14 Commission Principles (para 102):**
1. Open architecture → **lower** commission caps (disincentivize commission-led sales).
2. Closed architecture → **higher** caps (compensates for narrower product range). Note: IDEs (who can choose open) get lower caps than individuals (agents/associates) who are inherently closed.
3. Mandatory/near-mandatory products (e.g., motor TP, credit-linked insurance) → nil/very low caps (only documentation-cost recovery).
4. Complex/hard-to-sell products → higher caps than simple/easy products.
5. Under-served-area sales (towns ≤50k population + rural + cities ≤10 lakh) → **rewards over and above** normal caps.
6. Group insurance → lower commission than individual.
7. Renewals of high-demand/high-awareness products (e.g., health) → much lower than first-year.
8. Commission-led portability of health/motor → discouraged.
9. Life commission structure should incentivize **persistency** (multi-year payment) over first-year-only incentives.
10. Single-premium products and tax-incentive products → lower commissions.
11. Commercial insurance → lower, transparent commission (cascades into cost of doing business economy-wide).
12. **All-inclusive commission definition**: direct commission + incentives + awards + reimbursed selling expenses + related-party brand-value payments + non-cash gifts/trips — **any payment by any name** counts as commission.
13. PoSP commissions must come **out of** their sponsoring IDE's own commission (not additive).
14. Structure should move away from layered distribution toward **single-level** distribution.

**Box 4A — General Insurance Maximum Commission Limits (key figures):**
- **Motor TP**: Nil (IDE), 2.5% (Agents/Associates) — new vehicle. Old vehicle TP: 2.5% (IDE) / 5% (agents). Motor OD/PA/LL new vehicle: 5% (IDE) / 10% (agents); old: 10% (IDE) / 15% (agents).
- **Health individual**: first-time 15% (IDE) / 20% (agents) / 5% (hospitals); renewal 5%/10%; porting 5%/10%.
- **Health group** (employer & non-employer): first-time 2.5% (IDE, cap ₹100 lakh) / 5% (agents, cap ₹100 lakh) / 1% (hospitals, cap ₹25 lakh); renewal/porting same structure.
- **Marine-Cargo**: 10% (IDE) / 15% (agents). **Marine-Hull**: 10%/10%.
- **Misc-Retail** (PA/Travel/Homeowners/Shopkeepers/Liability/Cyber): 15%/17.5%. **Misc-Corporate/Group**: 10%/15%.
- **Government Schemes**: as notified in the scheme.
- **Property Retail**: first-time 15% (IDE)/20% (agents); renewal 10%/15%.
- **Property (PML-basis, slab-wise)**: SI<500cr → 7.5%/10%; 500-2500cr → 6.25%/7%; >2500cr → 5%/5.5%.
- **Engineering (EAR/CAR)**: same slab structure as property PML.
- **Insurance packaged with Loan/Credit** (lending-cum-distribution entities): Motor TP Nil; Motor OD/PA/LL 5%; Health first-time 5%/renewal 2.5%; Property single-premium 2.5%/multi-year 5%.
- **Rural/under-served bonus** (individual policies only): Category-I (towns/cities <10 lakh pop, other than Cat-II): +10% of applicable cap on first-year premium. Category-II (towns <50,000 pop + all rural): +20% of applicable cap.

**Box 4B — Life Insurance Maximum Commission Limits (key figures):**
- **Individual Non-Linked/Linked**, by Premium Payment Term (PPT): <5yr → 5% first-yr (IDE)/6.25% (agents), 2%/5% renewal; 5yr → 10%/12.5%, 2%/5%; 6-8yr → 14%/17.5%, 3%/5%; 9yr → 18%/22.5%, 3%/5%; 10yr+ → 20%/25%, 3%/5%.
  - Renewal escalator: from 6th policy year, +0.5% every 3 years — 5.5% (yrs 6-8) → 6% (yrs 9-11) → capped at **7% max**.
- **Individual Savings single premium**: 1% (IDE)/2% (agents), no renewal.
- **Individual Pure Term single premium**: 7.5%/10%. **Multi-year**: 25%/30% first-yr, 7.5%/10% renewal.
- **Group Pure Term single premium**: 1.5% (cap ₹20 lakh)/2% (cap ₹30 lakh). **Multi-year**: 5%/7.5% both years.
- **Pension/Annuity single premium**: 0.5%/0.75% (no commission for NPS annuity purchase). **Multi-year**: 5%/6.5% first-yr, 5% renewal both.
- **Group Fund Based**: 0.5% of yearly contribution, cap ₹10 lakh (IDE)/₹15 lakh (agents).
- **Life packaged with Loan/Credit**: Individual Pure Term single 2%; multi-year 2.5% first-yr/1% renewal. Group same structure.
- Same Category-I/II rural bonus (+10%/+20%) applies to individual life policies.

**Assurance of Good Market Conduct (paras 104–115):**
1. **Mandatory cost audit**: all insurers + IDEs with commission income >₹100 crore. Rationale explicitly stated: caps set a ceiling, audits verify what's actually counted toward it.
2. **Disclosure**: insurers must publish commission policy/structure on website; large distribution entities must disclose their regulated/agreed commission structure with insurers on website; complex/large commercial risk exceeding caps (due to risk-assessment cost) must show commission on the policy face; **all commercial policies with cover above ₹50 crore** must disclose commission on the policy document; records accessible to Insurance Information Bureau/PIR; insurer's Board Risk & Audit Committee must certify compliance; distribution entity's CEO/Board/Partners/Apex body must also certify compliance.
3. **Safeguards against indirect payments**: no outsourcing to Distributor-related parties without Board approval + confirmation of no business linkage + immediate regulator notification; related-party arrangements require advance regulator disclosure + website disclosure by both sides; both Boards must certify compliance with caps.

## Section 9 — Transparency in Pricing and Quality-Led Purchases (paras 116–130)

1. **Product/pricing/performance transparency:**
   - **Dark patterns explicitly prohibited** under insurance regulations (currently addressed only under Consumer Protection Act via CCPA guidelines) — e.g., requiring personal details before showing price.
   - Life/General Insurance Councils to develop **standard one-page information sheets + FAQ notes** per product class (policy wording legally supersedes the sheet).
   - Standard definitions for product performance/quality metrics to be issued — flags inconsistency: e.g., claim settlement ratio "by count" (includes partial/rejected/repudiated) vs the more meaningful "by amount"; grievances sometimes mis-recorded as service requests to avoid disclosure.
   - Access to product info/FAQ/brochure/rates/performance must be available **without requiring personal details** — "access to information should not be used as a lead generation opportunity."
   - **Board-approved commission policy (incl. rewards)** must be public, prominently placed, "not more than one click away from the landing page."

2. **Market conduct transparency:**
   - Direct verified customer-insurer connect before policy issuance — via OTP, Aadhaar face-auth, DoT number confirmation, deduplication to prevent third-party numbers.
   - "Know Your Insurer" / "Know Your Distributor" mechanisms via PIR.

3. **Money flow (Section 64VB alignment):**
   - Premium must move customer → insurer's account **directly** (no intermediate account except BASBA).
   - Reinsurer↔insurer funds (commission, ceding commission, claims) must flow directly, **not through broker's account**. Brokerage fee paid separately by insurer/reinsurer per engagement terms.
   - Premium payment only via UPI/Credit/Debit/customer's own bank account — **no third-party payments** (anti-fraud, anti-money-laundering rationale).
   - Claims paid to **verified** policyholder/nominee bank account (name-matched).

4. **Timely distributor payment:**
   - Commission must be paid **no later than the day after free-look period ends** (from date insurer receives premium); insurers should "strive" for 7-day payment.
   - Claw-back mechanisms allowed for cancelled policies.
   - Delayed payment attracts interest, chargeable to operating expenses.

## Section 10 — Safeguards Against Compulsory Bundling (paras 131–134)

- **Prohibits compulsory bundling** of insurance with bank/NBFC (IDE-registered) products/services. If a lender wants to insure its loan portfolio, it must take a **group policy as its own institutional expense**, not push it onto the borrower.
- **Acceptable package illustrations (safe list):**
  1. Complimentary group term insurance for depositors above a threshold.
  2. Complimentary group term insurance for borrowers.
  3. Credit card bundled with term/travel/accident insurance at no extra charge beyond normal card fee.
  4. Group health scheme inclusion at specified premium — **with mandatory disclosure of the bank's commission**.
  5. Conditional interest-rate discount for additional term life/property insurance security — **only if**: (a) customer shown both rates (with/without insurance), (b) no requirement to buy from that bank/NBFC specifically, (c) premium paid separately by customer (not folded into loan amount).
  - Explicitly discouraged: packaging critical-illness health cover with home/motor loans — "likely to be cost inefficient for borrowers."
- **MSME property insurance**: current ₹5 crore risk-cover limit to be **removed** — banks/NBFCs can offer property cover up to full property value (may exceed loan amount), subject to standard commission caps; pure protection life cover (if offered) capped at loan amount, at lower commission.
- Footnote definition: "compulsory bundling" = loan conditional on buying an insurance product (related or unrelated to the financed asset).

## Section 11 — Creating Awareness Against Mis-selling (paras 135–140)

1. **Suitability as enforceable obligation:** documented needs/suitability analysis mandatory for life sales above a defined ticket size, with audit trail. Customer consent/signature alone **does not absolve** the insurer/intermediary. Explicitly targets bank-led mis-presentation of savings/endowment products as fixed deposits.

**Box 5 — Mis-selling (adopts RBI's definition verbatim), covers:**
   - Sale unsuitable to customer profile (even with consent); sale without correct/complete info; sale without explicit consent; compulsory bundling; any other regulator-defined mis-selling.
   - **12 illustrative mis-selling scenarios** listed, including: selling regular-premium as single-premium; not explaining discontinuation consequences/low surrender value; selling term insurance to non-working-age/no-dependents customers; selling regular-premium to customers without steady income; selling ULIPs to risk-averse/retired customers without explaining mortality charges/risk; false assured-returns promises on Par/ULIP; presenting Non-Par as bank-deposit substitute with comparable/lower actual returns; presenting insurance as fixed-income/high-return investment; term insurance for non-earning dependents; inducing surrender/replacement on misleading return promises; high-value single-premium products with poor liquidity; life insurance sold purely for inheritance planning.
   - RBI's suitability/appropriateness, promotional-material, and anti-mis-selling-incentive directions to Regulated Entities are reproduced and adopted as guidance for insurance distribution too.

2. **Banning mis-selling incentive structures:** all remuneration (direct/indirect, monetary/non-monetary) folded into "commission" definition; **explicit prohibition on volume/reward-linked incentives for bank/NBFC staff** selling insurance (trips, luxury gifts, milestone bonuses, contest rewards named specifically as current common practice).

3. **Identifying individuals behind mis-sold policies:** functional identity of the specific seller tagged to each policy; mis-selling incidents placed in **public domain** via PIR — stated purposes: (1) enables objective disincentive from commission, (2) fixes accountability, (3) alerts future recruiters, (4) lets prospective customers see a seller's mis-selling history. **Mis-selling triggers commission claw-back.**

## Section 12 — Streamlining Motor Insurance (paras 141–142)

- Average motor commission **24%** (range 13–50%) despite TP being mandatory/simple.
- **OEM brokers + MISPs**: 30% combined market share (new+old vehicles); avg commission 24% (max 31%). FY25: ₹29,000 cr premium generated, **₹7,050 cr paid as commission**. New-vehicle TP is a "nil-effort" product (registration requires proof of insurance) yet OEM brokers/MISPs command 27–38% avg commission on new vehicles; even old-vehicle sales (low effort, existing garage relationship) draw ~12% avg.
- **NBFC/bank motor financiers** with compulsory loan-insurance bundling: 16% commission, borrower unaware.

**Box 6 — Motor Insurance:** Motor dealer-as-MISP's core business is vehicle sales, insurance is incidental — but dealer's point-of-sale leverage steers customer choice toward insurers paying higher commission, not customer benefit. FY23–25: motor premium +34%, motor commission **+259%**. Concern flagged: certain OEM-linked brokers sponsoring MISPs may pressure insurers against offering lower pricing via other channels, restricting cross-channel price competition. Also flags that sponsoring brokers have been "found many times wanting" in exercising oversight over their MISPs.

**Reform measures (para 142):**
1. Promote digital purchase via MII platforms (Bima Sugam) — platform fee capped at **≤5% of premium**.
2. MISP dealers meeting IDE legal structure (companies/LLPs/cooperatives/registered partnerships) **must register as IDE** if they want to keep selling insurance — can then sell multiple insurers' products.
3. MISP dealers **not** meeting IDE structure (proprietorship/unregistered partnership) must tie up as an IDE's PoSP, or become a single insurer's associate.
4. Specific obligations cast on motor-dealer IDEs/PoSPs via regulation (replacing current informal MISP guidelines) — violations attract regulatory consequences.
5. Obligations include: prominently display MII/QR-code purchase option; make new-vehicle customers aware of it; share customer mobile number with PIR (validated against VAHAN vehicle-registration system) — PIR sends a post-event direct message to the customer confirming compliance.
6. Expected conduct: **no denial of cashless repair** based on where insurance was bought; no OEM/IDE agreements (SLAs, performance-linked incentive plans) that reward dealers for insurance sales against policyholder interest.
7. Mandatory TP and easy-to-sell new-vehicle motor insurance to carry **much lower commissions** (reflected directly in Box 4A: Nil for TP-IDE).

## Section 13 — Market Infrastructure Institutions for Insurance (MII) (paras 143–147)

- Positioned as the "fully digital pull-based alternative" to push-based intermediary distribution — analogous to stock exchanges/depositories/UPI/credit bureaus in other financial sectors.
- **Bima Sugam** = first MII; all insurers bar three are shareholders in Bima Sugam India Federation; expected to be "fully operational" soon.
- More MIIs envisaged, promoted by different insurer groups, for competitive innovation. Objective: "maximize customer value subject to profitability for insurers."
- **Governance rules**: not-for-profit companies; **minimum 11 insurers** to establish one, no single insurer with significant beneficial ownership; at maturity each shareholder <10% ownership/voting; a "lead promoter" allowed up to 20% in formative years, must reduce to 15% in 5 years and <10% in 7 years.
- MIIs may get **preferred distribution status** but must run on a transparent fee structure "much lower than current distribution commission rates" — positioned as a level-playing-field, transparent-comparison platform for product features, pricing, claims settlement, grievance redressal.

## Section 14 — Public Insurance Registry (PIR) (paras 148–153)

- PIR = an MII promoted by **the Authority itself** (IRDAI) — the "Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) for Insurance," business-facing (vs Bima Sugam being customer-facing).
- Three stated goals: (1) growth & inclusion, (2) trust & transparency, (3) affordability & financial sustainability.
- Role: enabler for customer-facing MIIs (like Bima Sugam) to discover/verify/exchange insurance information; mitigates information asymmetry.
- Described as population-scale, interoperable, non-exclusionary — with **59 potential user stories** (full detail is in a *separate* PIR-specific consultation paper referenced by footnote, not reproduced here).
- Framed as the enabler shifting the sector's mindset from "insurance is sold" to "insurance is also bought" — supporting digital, DIY, tech-savvy population segments.

## Section 15 — Insurance Education and Awareness (paras 154–155)

- Short section: policyholder rights (info access on products/pricing/performance, insurer claim/grievance performance, distributor conduct) and obligations (timely risk disclosure, prudent risk management) framed as necessary complements to the reforms.
- Proposes using the recently established **Policyholder Education and Protection Fund (PEPF)** to run these awareness activities.

## Section 16 — Reforms Objectives, Measures, and their Assessment (paras 156–159)

- States these reforms **continue** the 2016/2023 reform lineage — "continuity with course correction," not a break.
- **Self-assessed coverage of the 10 Section-3 challenges:**
  - **Fully addressed (4):** regulatory arbitrage; commission-led (vs pricing/quality-led) sales; growth-first incentives; large-distributor leverage constraining insurer competition.
  - **Partially addressed (3):** misaligned insurer/distributor/public interests; high-cost/poor-service trust erosion; absent customer power.
  - **Groundwork laid, not fully addressed:** lack of transparency.
  - **Explicitly out of scope of this paper:** the overarching challenge of the sector's growth not matching the country's insurance need — paper states "reforms wider than the scope of this consultation paper are needed. They will be sequenced appropriately in the near future."
- References back to Section 1's objective-to-reform mapping table as the master tracker.
- Notes: "a multi-year effort requiring perseverance... full disclosure by all stakeholders, and assigning accountability to both regulated entities **and the regulator**."
